# A security alert

## Use this when

- An email arrives from any account's `hearth-ops-alerts` topic: one of the alarms A1 to A15 (A13 from C0.12) or the copy's intake alarm, a budget or cost anomaly alert, a GuardDuty or Access Analyzer finding, or an ECS deployment failure.
- The same alert arrives twice, once from the account's own topic (the fast path, within a minute or so) and once from management's (the assured path, within 5 to 15 minutes). A fast email stands for one call. An assured email stands for one episode, which may hold many calls: management's alarm emails as it enters ALARM and again as it returns to OK, and a matching call while it is already in ALARM emails nobody. Match each fast email with the assured episode that covers it (*Finding an assured alarm's calls*), whose span starts before its ALARM email, at its first breaching period; a fast email whose call the episode's search doesn't find is triaged on its own. An OK email needs no action of its own: it closes the episode's span.

## Who does what

- The person on call triages each alert within the working day, and at once for A1, A2, A6, A7, A15 and the copy's intake alarm.
- The DPO leads whenever personal data may be affected ([A suspected breach of personal data](breach.md)).
- An Owner answers for A3, A4 and A5, which are their own administration.
- An Owner, with `HearthOrgAdmin` in management, finds an assured alarm's calls for the person on call, who holds no set in management, and contains an unexpected session (both below).
- A repo admin turns GitHub Actions off for the repository when a GitHub role's session is unexpected (*Containing an unexpected session*).
- The second reviewer sees every alert in the quarterly review ([The quarterly review of break-glass uses](break-glass-review.md)).
- A coding agent never looks at production, an alert or its account.

## Before you start

- The email itself. A fast rule's names the alarm and the account, and: for A1 and A15, the call and its caller, the source identity the call set (an operator's sign-in name), the one the caller's session already carried (`inheritedSourceIdentity`), the reason tag and any error code. A chained `lz:pr-` deploy's A15 carries the person only as the inherited identity, and never the reason, since `cdk deploy`'s hop into a hearthbase role sends neither: its reason is in the A1 raised for the same session and in its open entry. A request that itself sets a source identity on a hearthbase role can come only from `hearth-break-glass`; for A7 and A8, the call and the caller; for A9, the finding's severity, type and id; for A10, the finding's resource type and id; for A14, the deployment and the service. An assured alarm's (`hearth-sec-a01-break-glass` to `hearth-sec-a15-hearthbase`, from management) names the alarm and, in its *Reason for State Change*, the datapoint that breached and when its period began, in UTC, but never a call; its calls are in `/hearth/security/org-trail` in management, found with the alarm's own filter patterns (*Finding an assured alarm's calls*, below). A12's is AWS Budgets' or Cost Anomaly Detection's own. Alerts carry ids and references only, never content.
- Who was on call, and what break-glass sessions were open (their open entries, [Reaching production: the break-glass role](break-glass.md)).
- The merged PRs, and, from C0.11 PR 4, GitHub's deployment history.
- For a GitHub role's trust or the RCP (A10, A15): `gh`, signed in, `jq`, a checkout of `main`, and the account ids an Owner shows, for [the quarterly review](break-glass-review.md)'s step 6 comparison. In a break-glass shell `gh` isn't signed in: its *Set up the comparison* says what to set there instead.

## Steps

Find the alert's row. If it isn't expected, contain it first (*Containing an unexpected session*, below), then follow [A suspected breach of personal data](breach.md).

### Finding an assured alarm's calls

The person on call holds no set in management, so an Owner does this, with `HearthOrgAdmin` there (A3 alarms on that session: note it with the alert).

1. Read the episode first: `aws cloudwatch describe-alarm-history --alarm-name hearth-sec-<the alarm, as a06-tampering> --history-item-type StateUpdate --query 'AlarmHistoryItems[].[Timestamp,HistorySummary,HistoryData]' --output text` gives each change of state: when it entered ALARM, when it returned to OK, and each change's data. In the change that entered ALARM, the data's `newState.stateReason` (the ALARM email's *Reason for State Change*) names the datapoint that breached and, in brackets, when its period began, in UTC, day first (`dd/mm/yy hh:mm:ss`); `newState.stateReasonData.evaluatedDatapoints` gives the same time in full. That is the episode's first breaching period, where its calls begin. The ALARM change, and its email, come later, never at the call: the copy receives a call 5 to 15 minutes after it is made, and the alarm changes state only once the period its datapoint fell in has been evaluated. The change back to OK, and its email, close the episode. CloudWatch keeps an alarm's history for 30 days only: for an older episode, the ALARM email's *Reason for State Change* gives the same time, and the OK email the end.
2. Read the alarm's filter patterns, giving its number: `aws logs describe-metric-filters --log-group-name /hearth/security/org-trail --filter-name-prefix hearth-sec-a06 --query 'metricFilters[].[filterName,filterPattern]' --output text`. A2 has two filters, A4 three and A6 ten, lettered `-a` onwards; every other alarm has one.
3. Search the copy with each pattern, as it is, from 5 minutes before the first breaching period step 1 read (15 for A11) until it returned to OK, or until now if it is still in ALARM, and again once it returns: `aws logs filter-log-events --log-group-name /hearth/security/org-trail --start-time <ms> --end-time <ms> --filter-pattern '<the pattern>' --query 'events[].message'`, or the pattern in the console's search of the log group, which takes the same syntax. Never start from the ALARM change's time: the call that raised it comes before. If the breaching period can't be read, start at least 25 minutes before the ALARM change (35 for A11): the copy's 15 minutes, one period, and 5 more. If a pattern finds no call, search further back, since CloudTrail doesn't promise when it delivers. If none is found even then (at least 25 minutes before the breaching period, 35 for A11, and on to now), or the alarm stays in ALARM, with no OK email, more than about an hour after its last matching call, treat it as an A6 raised in management: datapoints may have been put into `Hearth/Security` by hand, which the trail doesn't record, and they hold an alarm in ALARM, where a real match emails nobody. Cross-check over the episode with `aws cloudwatch get-metric-statistics --namespace Hearth/Security --metric-name <the alarm's metric> --statistics SampleCount --unit Count --period 300 --start-time <the episode's start> --end-time <now>` (`--period 900` for A11), the metric being what `aws cloudwatch describe-alarms --alarm-names <the alarm> --query 'MetricAlarms[].MetricName' --output text` prints. Each of the alarm's filters publishes one sample for each event it matches, so an event two of them find counts twice (a call to GitHub's OIDC provider, which A6 (b) and A6 (i) both find): compare a period's samples with the matches this step's searches found in it, added up over the alarm's patterns, never with the distinct calls. A period with more samples than those matches, or samples and no match, shows datapoints put in by hand. The times you search by are the copy's own, when it received each event; an event's `eventTime`, when the call was made, can be minutes earlier. Each event names its account (`recipientAccountId`), its call (`eventSource`, `eventName`), its caller (`userIdentity`) and any `errorCode`, and for A6 the filter that found it names the clause. Account for every call in that span, not only the first: several calls in one episode raise one email. An A3 episode raised by your own `HearthOrgAdmin` session is searched too, for any other session in it.
4. For A1, A2, A3, A15 and an A5 that moved an account, the review's saved queries list the same calls ([The quarterly review of break-glass uses](break-glass-review.md)), which the second reviewer, with `HearthSecurityReview`, can also run.

### Containing an unexpected session

Removing someone from a group stops only new sessions. Credentials AWS has already issued keep working until they expire: an hour for `hearth-break-glass`, `OrganizationAccountAccessRole` (OAAR), the GitHub roles and any role taken from one of them, an hour for `HearthOrgAdmin`, `HearthBreakGlassEntry` and `HearthBilling`, four for `HearthSecurityReview` and eight for `HearthNonprodOperator`. Until then a session can also take further roles, each with credentials of its own. IAM's own *Revoke active sessions* writes a policy onto the role, which the guardrails refuse on these roles in a member account. OAAR is fenced only while an account sits in its OU (`Security` or `Workloads`): in `Recovery`, or in the root before its set-up moves it, an OAAR session administers the account. So an Owner, with `HearthOrgAdmin` in management, in this order:

1. Stops new sessions: removes the person from the Identity Center group that gives them the set (the sets are assigned to groups), and, if their sign-in may be someone else's, disables the user and deletes their access-portal sessions (A4 alarms). A GitHub role's sessions (`hearth-gh-deploy`, `hearth-gh-diff`) come from workflow runs, not from a group: for one of those, a repo admin turns GitHub Actions off for the repository in its settings and cancels any run in progress, until the review says otherwise.
2. Contains management first, as below, if the session was there or came from there: an A3 for `HearthOrgAdmin`, `HearthSetUp` or OAAR, an A15 in management, or an A4 or A5 nobody planned. A live `HearthOrgAdmin` session could detach the SCP or move the account again.
3. If the account sits in `Recovery`, moves it back into its own OU at once (A5 alarms), even with a tier-2 change under way, which starts again after the review: `NoOaar` there denies every OAAR session from its next call, however new. An account still in the root for its set-up stays there, and step 4 covers its OAAR sessions.
4. Ends the account's live sessions with an SCP named `hearth-containment`, attached to that account alone (A5 alarms), never to the root or an OU. It denies every action on every resource where `aws:TokenIssueTime` is before its cut-off, in UTC (`DateLessThan`, for example `2026-11-01T02:10:00Z`), and `aws:PrincipalArn` is like `*:role/hearth-break-glass`, `*:role/OrganizationAccountAccessRole`, `*:role/hearth-gh-*`, `*:role/cdk-hearthbase-*`, `*:role/cdk-hearthapp-*` or `*:role/aws-reserved/sso.amazonaws.com/*` (`StringLike`). The cut-off is a few minutes after the moment you attach it (written ahead, and moved later if the attachment comes after it), never the time of step 1: a role the session took in between holds credentials issued after step 1, which an earlier cut-off would miss. It binds the person on call's own sessions in that account too, who sign in again after the cut-off. Never widen it to every role, which would stop the service's own tasks.
5. Looks in the trail for what the session made or changed in that account (*Finding an assured alarm's calls*, by the account and the caller): a stack, a role, a user or access key, a role's trust, an identity provider. A role or user it made, or a role whose trust it changed, gets a statement of its own in `hearth-containment` denying every action where `aws:PrincipalArn` is that role or user, with no time condition, until a reviewed change or tier 2 removes it. It also looks for a task or bastion the session started, and for a CloudFormation operation still running (each stack's events). A stack's operation runs under the execution role it was deployed with (the hearthbase one for a Baseline, the hearthapp one for a stage's stacks), which `hearth-containment` names, so its calls under credentials issued before the cut-off are refused: it may fail, or fail its rollback (`UPDATE_ROLLBACK_FAILED`). Note it: once containment ends, a reviewed `lz:pr-` deploy (or, for a stage's stack, the pipeline) continues or redoes it, since in `Workloads` only hearthbase's deploy role may continue a Baseline's rollback.
6. Detaches and deletes `hearth-containment` once every session its cut-off covers has expired, eight hours after the cut-off in non-prod, four in log-archive and one in prod, and once what step 5 found is removed. The times of step 1, the cut-off, the detachment and the deletion, and `hearth-containment`'s policy id, go in the on-call notes for the quarterly review.

`hearth-containment` is the one SCP a person makes in the console, and only for this; every lasting change is a reviewed PR.

No SCP binds management. For an unexpected session there, the Owner removes the person from the group as in step 1, then adds to that permission set's inline policy in Identity Center a statement denying every action on every resource where `aws:TokenIssueTime` is before a cut-off a few minutes after it is provisioned, and provisions the set (A4 alarms). If that set is `HearthOrgAdmin`, it ends the Owner's own session too, who signs in again after the cut-off. That statement binds only the set's own sessions, never a role one of them took, so the Owner, signed in again:

- Uses IAM's *Revoke active sessions* on each of management's `cdk-hearthbase-*` roles, which nothing refuses there. It ends every session of them issued before that moment, a running Foundation deploy's included, which may then fail as step 5 says.
- Finds in the trail each member account where the set's sessions assumed OAAR, or a role taken from it (A3 and A15, by `recipientAccountId`), and contains each as in steps 3 to 6.
- Looks for what the session made in management, as step 5 does, and removes it once the review has recorded it.

Once the set's sessions have expired, the Owner removes the statement the same way, and deletes the `AWSRevokeOlderSessions` policy the revocation wrote on each role.

For A2:

- An `AssumeRoot` session lasts at most 15 minutes, and its caller held `HearthOrgAdmin` in management: contain that caller as above, and add to the member account's `hearth-containment` (made as in step 4 if it has none) a statement denying every action where `aws:PrincipalArn` is like `*:root`, since no root user acts in a member account but for a planned task.
- The management root user is bound by no policy at all. Whoever holds its keys checks that both security keys are in their places, and if one isn't, or nobody explains the use, resets the root password through its role mailbox and registers new keys in place of the old. The DPO and the second reviewer are told.

## Check it worked

- Each alert is explained in the on-call notes, kept off GitHub.
- For an incident, the breach runbook's clocks were met.

## Afterwards

- Note each alert for the quarterly review.
- A filter, rule or alarm that needs changing is changed by a reviewed PR, never in the console.

## Don't

- Unsubscribe, mute or edit a topic, rule or alarm. The guardrails refuse a signed unsubscribe or a mute only in member accounts, where A6 alarms on the attempt. Management is bound by no guardrail: a `HearthOrgAdmin` or root session there that mutes A6 first, turning off the alarms' actions or muting them with a mute rule, or removing the topic or its subscription, is reported by nobody until the quarterly review reads the alarms and the subscription back and the trail's files show the calls (TECH-SPEC T24 item 5). The unsubscribe link at the foot of every alert email is unsigned, so no guardrail sees it: it fails only because the set-up confirmed each subscription with authentication (its step N), and a subscription confirmed from the email's link could be ended by it with no credentials. Never follow that link, and treat an email from AWS saying a subscription was cancelled as an A6: an alert path is gone.
- Forward an alert, to anyone or anywhere: it names an account, and its footer carries the subscription's unsubscribe link.
- Assume silence means all is well: when mail you expect doesn't come (a drill's A1, a budget's notice), treat it as a lost subscription and check it as the set-up's step N does, and have an Owner check that the trail still delivers to its copy (`aws cloudtrail get-trail-status`'s CloudWatch Logs fields, as the set-up runbook's *Check it worked* reads them): every assured alarm stays OK while the copy receives nothing, whatever happens. When an assured email you expect doesn't come (the A3 for an Owner's own session, say), also have the Owner read `aws cloudwatch describe-alarms --alarm-name-prefix hearth-sec- --query 'MetricAlarms[].[AlarmName,StateValue,StateUpdatedTimestamp]' --output text`: an assured alarm already in ALARM emails nobody about a new match, and one in ALARM that no open episode explains is an A6, held up by datapoints put in by hand.
- Ask a coding agent to look at production or at an alert.
- Add an SMS subscription.
- Make or change an SCP or a permission set in the console, but for *Containing an unexpected session* (and the set-up's own `HearthSetUp`, made and deleted by [Setting up the AWS organisation and its accounts](aws-account-set-up.md)).
